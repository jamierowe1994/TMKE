# TMKE Voice & Writing Guide
### A practical reference for training, Member Hub content and marketing copy

## 01. Our voice

**Professional, conversational, clear and genuinely helpful.**

We write like someone who understands the subject and is explaining it to another person, not like a software manual, corporate handbook or sales presentation.

Our copy should feel:

- **Knowledgeable:** We understand the tools, the property industry and why things matter.
- **Approachable:** We use everyday language and speak directly to the reader.
- **Considered:** Every explanation gives the reader enough context to understand what they're doing.
- **Practical:** We focus on what someone needs to know, what they can do and what happens next.
- **Confident:** We explain things without overselling them or constantly trying to sound impressive.

The aim is to make complicated things feel straightforward without treating the reader like a beginner.

## 02. Explain what, why and how

One of the biggest improvements in our revised training copy is the explanation behind an instruction.

Don't just tell someone what a feature does. Where useful, explain why it matters and what it means for them.

**Before**

"The report pulls together all the client data into one document."

**TMKE style**

"The Monthly Management Report brings together the performance of every client account into one document, giving the management team a clear overview of how our social media services are performing across the business."

The second version explains the feature and its purpose.

**Our rule:** Give enough context to make the information meaningful. Don't add a longer explanation when a short instruction already does the job.

## 03. Introduce information before listing it

Avoid dropping readers straight into a list of features, steps or options.

Use a short introductory sentence to establish what they're about to read and why it's relevant.

**Before**

"Content, Audience and Trends"

- Content
- Audience
- Trends

**TMKE style**

"These sections give you a more detailed understanding of how the client's social media accounts are performing:"

- **Content:** A breakdown of the month's posts and reels, including their performance and best-performing content.
- **Audience:** Insights into who follows the account, including audience demographics and locations.
- **Trends:** A comparison of recent performance, helping you identify changes and patterns over time.

**Our rule:** Lists should expand on an explanation, not replace one.

## 04. Write to the person using the platform

Our training should feel like we're guiding someone through an experience.

Use **you**, **your**, **we** and **our** naturally. Describe what the reader will see and what they can do next.

| Instead of | Write |
|---|---|
| It asks whether to regenerate the commentary. | You'll be asked whether you'd like to regenerate the commentary. |
| The user selects the month. | Select the month you'd like to report on. |
| The system displays a warning. | You'll see a warning before publishing. |
| The client report is accessible in the Hub. | Clients can access their report through their Member Hub. |

Avoid unnecessary passive language and referring to people as "users" when speaking directly to them.

**Our rule:** Describe the experience from the reader's perspective, not the software's perspective.

## 05. Give every section a clear structure

Our training copy generally works best with four elements:

**1. A clear heading**

Tell the reader exactly what the section covers.

**2. A short introduction**

Explain what the feature, process or section is for.

**3. The practical detail**

Walk through the steps, options or information in a logical order.

**4. The useful takeaway**

Explain what happens next, what to check or anything important to remember.

Not every section needs all four. A simple instruction might need one sentence. A complicated process might need several paragraphs.

**Our rule:** Structure should follow the complexity of the subject, not a fixed word count.

## 06. Use consistent labels and formatting

For lists describing features, options or categories, use **bold labels followed by a colon**.

For example:

- **Member Hub notification:** Clients receive a notification when their report is ready.
- **Email notification:** You can also choose to send an email with a direct link.
- **Taking down a report:** Hides the published report until you're ready to make it available again.

Keep headings short and descriptive. Use the exact names of buttons, tabs and features shown in the platform.

Use numbered steps for a sequence that must be completed in order. Use bullet points for options, explanations and things to check.

Keep paragraphs relatively short, especially in Member Hub training where content appears alongside screenshots and interactive walkthroughs.

## 07. Use call-outs when something deserves attention

Some information is too important to bury in a paragraph.

Use a short call-out when there is a genuine risk, an easily missed detail or a reason behind a particular process.

**Important:** For something the reader must know before continuing, particularly where an action could affect a client or published content.

**Be careful:** For actions that could overwrite, remove or unintentionally change existing work.

**Why it matters:** For explaining the reasoning behind a process or decision.

**TMKE tip:** For practical advice that helps someone get more value from a feature or improve their marketing.

Example:

**Be careful:** Redrafting replaces any changes you've already made. If you need to regenerate the commentary, do so before starting your manual edits.

**Our rule:** Call-outs should earn their place. Don't turn ordinary information into a warning.

## 08. Make instructions feel natural

Training instructions should be precise, but they don't need to sound mechanical.

**Before**

"Click Republish. Select the relevant option. Confirm."

**TMKE style**

"Click **Republish** and choose how you'd like to update the report."

Then explain the available options.

Use language such as:

- "Once you've..."
- "You'll see..."
- "You can also..."
- "If you need to..."
- "This allows you to..."
- "Before publishing, check..."
- "When you're ready..."

Vary the sentence openings. Repeating "You'll" or "This allows you to" throughout a page can make the copy sound formulaic.

**Our rule:** Write clear instructions first, then make them sound like something a person would naturally say.

## 09. Be specific without overexplaining

Replace vague claims with actual information.

Avoid statements such as:

- "Makes everything easier."
- "Takes your marketing to the next level."
- "A powerful tool for your business."
- "Helps you achieve better results."

Explain *how* something helps instead.

**Before**

"This feature makes managing reports easier."

**TMKE style**

"Rather than reviewing each client report individually, management can see the key figures, insights and plans for every account in one place."

The benefit is clear because the explanation is specific.

**Our rule:** Show the value through what the feature actually does. Don't rely on generic marketing language.

## 10. Match the tone to the audience

The core TMKE voice stays consistent, but the delivery changes depending on where the copy appears.

### Internal training

Clear, instructional and thorough enough to prevent mistakes.

Explain the reasoning behind important processes, distinguish internal information from client-facing content and make responsibilities clear.

### Member Hub training

Friendly, encouraging and practical.

Explain what members can achieve, introduce features in a natural order and avoid overwhelming them with technical details. The course explains why; interactive walkthroughs show where and how.

### Website and service copy

More concise, benefit-led and commercially focused.

Lead with what matters to estate agents. Explain the service clearly and avoid exaggerated promises.

### Social media

More personality, shorter sentences and a little cheekiness where appropriate.

We can be candid, playful and opinionated, but never at the expense of clarity. A social caption should sound like something we'd actually say.

**Our rule:** Same personality, different level of detail.

## 11. Language and editorial conventions

- Use British English throughout.
- Use contractions naturally: "you'll", "we're", "don't", "it's".
- Capitalise official product names, feature names and interface labels consistently.
- Use sentence case for ordinary headings.
- Avoid unnecessary jargon. Explain technical terms when readers genuinely need to understand them.
- Avoid exaggerated claims, empty enthusiasm and excessive exclamation marks.
- Don't promise outcomes that cannot be guaranteed.
- Don't invent functionality, processes or benefits.
- Avoid repeating the same point in the introduction, list and conclusion.
- Use punctuation naturally, favouring commas and full stops over long dashes.
- Keep examples relevant to UK estate agents and property marketing wherever appropriate.

## 12. Our editing checklist

Before approving any TMKE copy, ask:

1. **Is it clear?** Can someone understand the point without reading it twice?
2. **Is there enough context?** Have we explained why something matters where necessary?
3. **Does it sound human?** Would we actually explain it this way in conversation?
4. **Is it useful?** Does each paragraph tell the reader something they need to know?
5. **Is it structured properly?** Do headings, introductions and lists flow naturally?
6. **Is it accurate?** Are the feature names, actions and outcomes correct?
7. **Is it the right length?** Have we explained enough without repeating ourselves?
8. **Does it suit the audience?** Internal staff, members and potential clients need different levels of detail.

## The TMKE writing principle

**Explain things properly. Say them naturally. Make every sentence useful.**

We don't need to make everything shorter, longer, friendlier or more impressive.

We need to make it clearer, more relevant and easier for the person reading it to understand what matters and what to do next.

## Appendix: patterns from the rewrites

Not part of Danielle's guide above, which takes precedence. These are things she
changed consistently in drafts (the training courses, the Reporting training and
the Content Calendar plan, October 2026) that the guide doesn't spell out.

### Headings that make a point

Section headings in plans and pages say something, conversationally, rather than
label a topic:

- "Your content, sorted." (was "The Content Calendar.")
- "Three ways to get it done" (was "Three paid levels")
- "From content to calendar" (was "Calendar to planner")
- "Build it once" (was "One month builder")
- "One system, two ways to pay" (was "Card or brand")
- "Two calendars, two different jobs" · "What it costs, and what you get"

Course and lesson titles stay in Title Case ("A Rhythm You Can Keep"). Email headings
are Title Case with a full stop; email subjects are sentence case with a full stop.

### Plain names, never internal ones

- No code, field or file names in reader copy. Say what the thing is.
- Name people and businesses as the reader knows them: "independent agents", not
  "external agents"; "The Lettings Experts" in full.
- "Around 20 posts a month" rather than false precision, unless the exact number is
  the point.

### Full sentences, not notes

Clipped notes read like a specification. "Replaces Canva plus HighLevel uploads"
became "No more building the same month in Canva and uploading it page by page to
HighLevel." A short line to land a point still has its place: "They're not the same
thing, and that's the point."

### Reassure where people worry

"Nothing in the walkthrough will make changes to your account." "Your designs and
calendar stay as they are." One line, where a reader might hesitate.

### Group checks under plain labels

A list of things to check reads best under category labels (**Accuracy:**,
**Context:**, **Recommendations:**, **Comparisons:**, **New accounts:**, **Tone of
voice:**, **Presentation:**, **Headline metrics:**), each followed by the rule as a
full sentence.

### House rules that still apply

- No em dashes or long dashes, and no italics for emphasis.
- Walkthrough cards say where and how, one idea each, with no aphorisms.
- Client-facing report copy also follows docs/smm-report-rules.md.
- The wine subheading strip is a design element, not copy: its words are short
  and in the house style.
