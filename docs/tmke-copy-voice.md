# TMKE copy voice

How TMKE writes, taken from Danielle's own rewrites (21 Oct 2026): the Member Hub
training courses, the Reporting training for the team, and the Content Calendar
plan. Every rule below is something she changed, again and again, in drafts that
were accurate but didn't sound like TMKE. Use it for anything a person reads:
training, page copy, emails, plans, captions, notices.

The facts come first and are never bent to fit the voice. But accurate is not
the same as finished: a draft that only mirrors the screen still needs this pass.

## The voice in one line

A knowledgeable colleague explaining something properly: warm, clear and
confident, never salesy, never clipped.

## 1. Say why before how

Open a section with what the thing is for, then how to use it.

- Mine: "Overview, Content, Audience and Trends are our working figures."
- Hers: "The Client Commentary tab contains the written insights and recommendations
  that appear in the client's monthly report." Then the detail.
- In lists, finish the thought with what it does for the reader: "A comparison of
  the current month's performance against the previous two months, *helping you
  identify changes and patterns over time*."

## 2. Write full sentences, not fragments

Clipped notes read like a spec. Expand them into what a person would say.

- Mine: "Replaces Canva plus HighLevel uploads."
- Hers: "No more building the same month in Canva and uploading it page by page to
  HighLevel."
- Mine: "No invented reasons." / Hers: "Only explain why something happened when
  there's evidence or relevant information to support it."

But keep it lean: one idea per paragraph, two or three sentences, and break a long
run-on into short paragraphs rather than one block.

## 3. Talk to the reader

"You" and "your" for the reader; "we", "us" and "our team" for TMKE. Contractions
are welcome (you'll, we're, don't, it's). Prefer "you'll be asked whether you'd like
to…" over "it asks whether…": the person, not the system, is the subject.

## 4. Headings that say something

Headings carry a benefit or a point, conversationally, not a label.

- "Your content, sorted." · "Three ways to get it done" · "From content to calendar"
  · "Build it once" · "One system, two ways to pay" · "Two calendars, two different
  jobs" · "What it costs, and what you get"
- Course and lesson titles stay in Title Case ("A Rhythm You Can Keep"); section
  headings in plans and pages are sentence case. Email headings: Title Case with a
  full stop; subjects sentence case with a full stop.

## 5. Lists: lead in, label, explain

- Introduce a list with a sentence ending in a colon ("Each recommendation includes
  four key areas:").
- Each item: a **bold label and colon**, then a full sentence ("**Accuracy:** Every
  figure must match the report. Don't estimate results or exaggerate performance.").
- Group checks under plain category labels (Accuracy, Context, Recommendations,
  Comparisons, Tone of voice, Presentation) rather than punchy one-liners.

## 6. Call-outs

Use them sparingly, bold, at the start of the line:

- **Tip.** Helpful, optional.
- **Important:** Something that goes wrong if missed ("Don't publish without client
  commentary.").
- **Be careful:** An action that can't be undone or loses work.

## 7. Plain names, never internal ones

- No code, field or file names in reader copy (details.zernio, PLAN_LINKED,
  smm_leads). Say what it is.
- Name things as the reader knows them: "independent agents", not "external
  agents"; "The Lettings Experts" in full; "Client Commentary", "Management
  Summary", "Member Hub" with capitals as on screen.
- Button and screen names exactly as they appear, in bold: **Publish to client**,
  **Pull monthly report**.

## 8. Rhythm and reassurance

- A short line to land a point: "They're not the same thing, and that's the
  point." · "It's one button."
- Reassure where people worry: "Nothing in the walkthrough will make changes to
  your account." · "Your designs and calendar stay as they are."
- Benefit pairs, sparingly: "Less time wondering what to post, more time getting it
  done."
- "Around 20 posts" rather than false precision, unless the number is the point.

## 9. House rules (unchanged)

- British English. No em dashes; use a colon, a comma or a full stop. No
  exclamation marks. No italics for emphasis.
- Professional and conversational, never hype or "over-the-top sales language".
- Courses explain why; walkthrough cards say where and how, one idea each, no
  aphorisms.
- Client-facing report copy follows docs/smm-report-rules.md.
